TWO-ROVER ROOM MAPPING - ALGORITHM

Lines marked FIX are corrections to problems in the original draft.
Lines marked SUGGESTION are optional improvements.
Open decisions are collected at the end.


PHASE 0 - START POINT

Both rovers are placed side by side against the same wall.

Each rover carries a tag (ArUco / AprilTag). Each reads the other's tag, which
gives the distance between them and their relative orientation in one step. The
start point is the midpoint between them, and becomes the origin of the shared
map.

Tags beat measuring by hand: distance and angle together, and the scale is
trustworthy because the stereo camera is calibrated (baseline known to 148.9 mm,
0.5 percent).

FIX - the rovers cannot read each other's tags while facing their travel
directions. They drive off in opposite directions, so they face away from each
other. The order must be:
    1. place them facing each other
    2. read tags, fix the start point
    3. each rotates to its travel heading
    4. then start driving
  Or mount a tag on more than one side of each rover so it can be read from any
  angle. Pick one before building this.


PHASE 1 - PERIMETER, DRIVING IN OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS

Each rover follows the wall at 30 cm, one clockwise and one counter-clockwise.
The side sonar it follows decides its direction:
    follows the wall on its RIGHT -> travels counter-clockwise
    follows the wall on its LEFT  -> travels clockwise

Control loop: keep the side sonar at 30 cm, steer in proportion to the error.
Too close, steer away. Too far, steer toward.

CORNERS

Inner corner (concave, the room turning in on itself):
    front sonar drops to 30 cm, the way ahead is blocked
    -> turn AWAY from the followed wall
       (following the right wall means turning left)

Outer corner (convex, the wall ending and turning away):
    the side sonar grows past 30 cm, the wall has ended
    -> turn TOWARD the followed wall and drive around it

FIX - an outer corner and a doorway are identical to the sonar. Both make the
side reading jump above 30 cm. Treated as a corner, the rover turns into the
doorway and drives out of the room.
  After the reading jumps, carry straight on for a set distance, 50 to 80 cm. If
  the wall comes back, it was a gap or a doorway, so continue. If it does not, it
  was a real outer corner, so turn.

FIX - reacting at 30 cm from the front is too late at speed, since 30 cm at full
speed is a collision. Either use a larger front threshold than the side one, or
slow down as the front reading drops.

NOTE - "perimeter" means the drivable outline, not the walls. A desk against a
wall becomes part of the perimeter and the rover drives around it. That is
expected, but the enclosed area is then smaller than the room's true floor area.

SUGGESTION - record the driven path as a list of points. It is the most reliable
data in the whole run, because it was driven at a known 30 cm from the wall, and
Phase 6 uses it to check the finished map.


PHASE 2 - END POINT

The rovers meet on the far side. Each reads the other's tag again, and that
meeting is the end point.

This works naturally, unlike at the start: they approach head-on, so they are
already facing each other.

The meeting gives two things:
  - the end point, closing the perimeter into a loop
  - a drift measurement. Estimates have drifted on the way round, so the gap
    between where each rover thinks it is and where the tags say it is is exactly
    the accumulated error. Spread that correction back along the path each rover
    drove, rather than jumping it all at the meeting point.


PHASE 3 - SPLIT THE ROOM IN TWO

*** THIS PHASE IS REWRITTEN. The original plan was to cut from the start point
to the end point. That does not give equal halves. ***

WHY THE START-TO-END LINE FAILS

Two separate problems:

1. The rovers do not drive equal halves of the perimeter. They meet when their
   COMBINED distance equals the total perimeter, not when each has done half. If
   one side has more corners, or one rover runs slightly slower, the split can
   easily be 70/30 rather than 50/50.

2. Even with a perfectly equal perimeter split, the interior areas can be very
   unequal. Area does not follow boundary length. A long thin alcove adds a lot
   of perimeter and almost no area, and a wide open span adds a lot of area with
   very little perimeter. In an L-shaped room the start-to-end line can be badly
   lopsided even when both rovers drove exactly the same distance.

AND THE THING TO BALANCE IS NOT TOTAL AREA

By the time the rovers split, the perimeter strip is already mapped. What is left
is the unexplored middle. So the split should balance the UNKNOWN cells, which is
the actual remaining work, not the total floor area.

THE FIX - BALANCE BY UNKNOWN AREA

1. Close the perimeter into a polygon from the two driven paths.

2. Mark every grid cell inside the polygon. Cells already mapped during the
   perimeter drive are done. The rest are unknown, and they are the work to be
   shared.

3. Find the polygon's longest axis, and make the dividing line PERPENDICULAR to
   it. Cutting across the long direction gives two compact halves. Cutting along
   it gives two long thin slivers, which are slower to explore and full of
   awkward turns.

4. Slide that line along the long axis until the count of unknown cells on each
   side is equal. Do not use area, and do not use perimeter length. Count cells,
   which works for any room shape without any geometry maths.

5. Assign each half to whichever rover drove more of the boundary next to it.
   That keeps each rover working near ground it already knows and avoids sending
   both of them across the room.

6. Check both halves are fully reachable from their own side. Flood-fill each
   half from its rover's entry point and confirm every unknown cell in it can be
   reached without crossing the dividing line.

FALLBACK FOR AWKWARD ROOMS

Step 6 fails when a straight line cuts off a pocket, which is common in
L-shaped and U-shaped rooms. In that case do not use a straight line at all:

  - flood outward from both rovers at the same time, one step at a time, through
    free space only, so walls are respected
  - each unknown cell belongs to whichever flood reaches it first
  - both rovers are at the same place at the meeting point, so give one flood a
    head start along its own driven arc, otherwise the split is meaningless
  - if the two counts end up uneven, adjust the head start and flood again

This gives two regions that are always fully reachable, always respect walls, and
can be balanced by adjusting one number. It costs a little more code than a
straight line, but it cannot produce an unreachable pocket.

SUGGESTION - build the straight-line version first and keep the flood version for
when a real room breaks it. Log the two unknown-cell counts every run, so it is
obvious when the split is going wrong.

CHECKS
  - the two unknown-cell counts are within a few percent of each other
  - every unknown cell is reachable by its assigned rover
  - neither half is a thin sliver: check the ratio of its width to its length


PHASE 4 - FRONTIER EXPLORATION INSIDE EACH HALF

Each rover fills in its own half:
  - a frontier is the edge between mapped-clear space and unknown space
  - group nearby frontier cells together
  - pick one by cost against benefit, near and large beats far and small
  - drive to it, map what is revealed, repeat
  - stop when no reachable frontier is left inside the assigned half

Neither rover crosses the dividing line.

DECISION - what does the rover that finishes first do?
    a) wait where it is
    b) return to the start point
    c) cross over and help finish the other half
  Option (c) is the most useful and the most work: the dividing line has to be
  dropped safely and both rovers must avoid re-covering the same ground. Option
  (a) is simplest and fine for a first working version.

SUGGESTION - balancing by unknown cells in Phase 3 should already make the two
rovers finish at roughly the same time, which makes this decision much less
important than it would otherwise be.

As soon as a rover finishes its half, it sends its fingerprint to the laptop.

PHASE 6 - MAP MERGING

The laptop has both halves and joins them into one map.

1. Coarse alignment, from the tags.
   The start point and the end point are both measured from tag readings, so the
   two halves already sit in the same coordinate system. This is the main
   alignment and it needs no image matching at all.

2. Fine alignment, where the halves overlap.
   Both rovers drove the perimeter, so both saw the area near the start point and
   near the end point. Drift during Phase 4 can leave those overlaps slightly
   off. Slide and rotate one half by a small amount until the overlapping walls
   line up best.
   Keep the search small, a few tens of cm and a few degrees. If the best fit is
   larger than that, call it a failure rather than accepting it - a large
   correction means something else went wrong, and accepting it hides the real
   fault.

3. Fuse the cell scores.
   Both halves are grids of scores, positive for wall and negative for clear. Add
   them cell by cell. Where they overlap and agree the score strengthens. Where
   they disagree they partly cancel, which is correct: neither rover overrules
   the other.

4. Check against the driven path.
   The perimeter is the most reliable data in the run, because it was driven at a
   known 30 cm from the wall. The merged outline should match the driven path,
   offset by 30 cm. If it does not, the merge is wrong, and this is the cheapest
   way to find that out.

5. Output one map.
   One grid, one origin, one cell size, with the perimeter and both halves in it.
   Save it with the origin and cell size recorded, so it can be reopened later.

CHECKS FOR THE WHOLE MERGE
  - the outline closes with no step or kink where the rovers met
  - a corner seen by both rovers appears once, not as two offset copies
  - the outline length matches the room measured with a tape
  - the mapped area is close to the room's real area


WHAT IS THE FINGERPRINT?

Phase 5 says a fingerprint goes to the laptop and is used to map the two halves,
but not what it contains. Guessing wrong means building the wrong thing.

Most likely meanings:

  a) A visual place signature. A compact description of what a place looks like,
     so the laptop can tell both rovers saw the same physical spot. This is the
     standard tool for joining two robots' maps.

  b) The perimeter outline itself, used as a shape signature to match the halves.

  c) The local map, compressed. In which case "fingerprint" just means "the map
     data" and the merge is Phase 6 above, with nothing extra to build.

WORTH KNOWING - with the start point AND the end point both measured from tags,
the halves are already aligned. A fingerprint is not needed to align them. It
would be a cross-check, or a way to fix drift built up during Phase 4.
If that is the intent, (a) is the right reading and Phase 5 is real work. If it
is (c), Phase 5 is just "send the map".


OPEN DECISIONS

1. Tags at the start: rotate after reading, or mount tags on several sides?
2. Outer corner versus doorway: how far to drive straight before committing?
3. Front sonar threshold, and does the rover slow as it approaches?
4. Phase 3: start with the straight-line split, or go straight to the flood split?
5. What does the rover that finishes its half first do?
6. What exactly is the fingerprint?
